Difficult access concrete bases

Installing Concrete Bases With Difficult Access In Slough

We install concrete bases in Slough where getting materials and equipment into the garden is not straightforward. Narrow side passages, restricted gates and tight garden layouts can all affect how the work needs to be planned.

That does not mean the installation has to be compromised. We assess the access before starting, work out how excavation material and concrete will be moved, and organise the job so the base can still be properly prepared and finished.

For a new garden shed or other small garden building, the aim is to leave a solid, level foundation that will continue supporting the structure properly rather than relying on a quick slab over poorly prepared ground.

Typical difficult-access projects

  • Garden sheds behind houses with narrow side access
  • Small garden buildings where machinery access is restricted
  • Gardens reached through narrow pathways or gates
  • New sheds being installed on tightly enclosed garden plots
  • Concrete bases where excavation spoil needs to be removed through the property
The installation itself

A Difficult Access Site Still Needs Proper Ground Preparation

Restricted access changes the logistics of the job, not the standard of preparation. We still excavate to the required depth, remove unsuitable material and prepare the formation before the concrete is placed.

Where the existing ground is soft, inconsistent or affected by previous landscaping, we deal with that before moving on. A compacted sub-base is installed where required to create a stable platform beneath the concrete.

We then set the formwork accurately, check levels and allow for appropriate drainage and fall. The finished base needs to be level for the building while also being positioned correctly in relation to the surrounding ground.

For suitable applications, reinforcement can also be incorporated into the slab where the expected loading and ground conditions make it appropriate. The specification is based on the actual installation rather than using exactly the same approach for every garden.

Access and logistics

Planning The Concrete Pour When Access Is Restricted

The biggest difference on a difficult-access job is usually getting everything to the work area efficiently. Before the installation, we consider the width of the access route, gate dimensions, distance from the road and where excavation spoil can be placed or removed.

Concrete supply is planned around those restrictions. Depending on the site, concrete may need to be moved from the delivery point to the base location using suitable equipment or controlled manual handling rather than assuming a standard mixer can simply pull up beside the garden.

If a batching plant or concrete supplier is being used for the pour, we coordinate the delivery timing so the concrete arrives when the excavation, sub-base and formwork are ready. This helps keep the pour controlled and avoids leaving a prepared site waiting unnecessarily.

We also protect the route through the property as far as reasonably possible and keep material movement organised. Tight access rewards good planning because there is much less room for last-minute changes once the concrete is on site.

Installation process

How We Install A Difficult Access Concrete Base In Slough

1. Assess the access and proposed base position

We look at the garden, access route, existing ground and location of the new building. This gives us a clear idea of what can be brought through the property and how the installation needs to be organised.

2. Set out the base

We mark out the required footprint and check its position against the garden, boundaries and proposed building. Getting this right before excavation prevents problems later when the shed is delivered.

3. Excavate the ground

The required area is excavated and unsuitable material is removed. On a tight garden, excavation needs to be controlled so we do not unnecessarily disturb surrounding paving, planting or usable garden areas.

4. Prepare and compact the sub-base

Where the ground requires it, we install a suitable sub-base and compact it properly. This is one of the most important parts of the installation because the concrete can only perform as well as the ground supporting it.

5. Install formwork and check levels

The formwork is positioned to establish the final dimensions and levels. We check the fall and surrounding levels so water is not unnecessarily directed towards the garden building.

6. Arrange the concrete supply and pour

Once everything underneath is ready, the concrete is brought onto site according to the access plan. We place and distribute it carefully, incorporating reinforcement where the specification calls for it.

7. Level and finish the base

The concrete is levelled and finished to provide a suitable surface for the garden building. We leave the installation tidy and allow the concrete to gain the required strength before the structure is loaded onto it.

Long-term performance

Why The Preparation Matters More On A Difficult Access Site

It can be tempting to focus on the concrete itself because that is the part you see at the end. In practice, much of the long-term performance comes from what happens underneath it.

If loose or unsuitable ground is left beneath the slab, the base can settle as the ground changes. Once a shed has been installed, correcting that problem becomes considerably more disruptive because the building may need to be emptied, moved or dismantled before the base can be repaired.

That is why we do not cut corners simply because access is awkward. The excavation, sub-base, compaction, levels and drainage all need to be considered before the concrete goes down.

Common installation problem

Poor Sub-Base Preparation Can Lead To A Sinking Shed Base

One of the problems we are careful to avoid is placing concrete over a poorly prepared sub-base. If the material underneath is loose, inconsistent or unable to support the load properly, sections of the slab can settle differently over time.

That movement can leave the shed floor uneven, put stress on parts of the building and create gaps where water can collect around the structure.

On a difficult-access project, it is particularly important to get the preparation right because repairing a failed base later can be far more involved than preparing it properly in the first place.

Problems we aim to prevent

  • Sinking or settlement beneath sections of the concrete
  • An uneven surface beneath the garden building
  • Cracking caused by movement or inadequate support
  • Poor drainage around the finished base
  • Expensive disruption if the building has to be moved later
