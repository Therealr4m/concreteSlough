Can You Build a Concrete Base on a Sloping Garden in Slough?

Yes, you can build a concrete base on a sloping garden in Slough. For a shed or garden office, the finished slab should be level and supported across its full footprint. A gentle slope can often be dealt with during ground preparation. A bigger drop may call for a retained platform or a different foundation design.

How to measure the slope where the base will sit

Check the fall across the proposed footprint. A garden that looks steep from the house may have a fairly level corner, while a spot beside a patio can slope in two directions. Mark out the corners and use a laser level or a level string line as a reference. Measure down from the same level to the ground at each corner. The difference between the largest and smallest readings gives the fall across those points. Check the middle too, especially if there is a dip, mound or old flower bed inside the footprint.

For example, a fall of 150 mm across a 3 m shed footprint means the low corner is 150 mm below the high corner. That figure tells you how much the ground level varies. The excavation depth also depends on the chosen height of the finished base, the slab and sub-base design, and how far down suitable ground is found. On a narrow plot, turning a rectangular shed so its shorter side runs downhill can reduce the fall across the footprint, if there is still room for the door and access around the building.

When to cut into the slope or raise the low side

Cutting into the high side

On a modest slope, the footprint can often be excavated into the higher ground so the slab sits closer to the low side of the garden. Turf, topsoil and unsuitable material are removed to reach suitable ground, and a compacted sub-base provides an even platform for the slab. Cutting deeper on the high side leaves an uphill bank. If that bank needs support, or if the cut is close to a wall or another structure, the excavation and any retaining work need to be considered together.

Raising the low side

Where some build-up is needed, suitable engineered fill can form part of the platform if it is placed and compacted in layers and the edge is adequately supported. Loose topsoil or garden spoil can settle under the slab. Uneven settlement can leave one corner unsupported, crack the base or cause the shed door to bind. Temporary timber formwork shapes the concrete during the pour. Any fill that needs permanent restraint requires separate support as part of the design.

A steep drop, soft ground, deep old fill or an existing retaining wall can change what is suitable. The weight of a new slab and building can add load to a wall below it, even if the base sits back from the edge. A wall that already leans, bulges or shows significant cracking is a reason to have the ground and wall assessed before excavation or loading. Reinforcement in a slab can help control cracking when it is part of the design, but the ground underneath still has to support the slab. These are the same ground support considerations that apply to concrete bases on uneven ground.

Drainage and access around the finished base

Look at the proposed position after rain. Water may already cross the footprint on its way downhill, and a new slab can interrupt that route. Cutting into the slope can also leave the uphill side of the shed against wet ground. The ground around the base needs a route for runoff that keeps water away from the building and avoids sending it towards a neighbour's property. An uphill drain only helps if it has a suitable outlet. A gravel strip can still fill with water if there is nowhere for it to go.

Match the slab footprint to the building supplier's foundation detail. An exposed strip of slab on the uphill side can collect rain and direct it towards the wall unless the edge detail deals with runoff. Check the door position against the proposed slab height too. A door on the downhill side may end up well above the path, while a door facing uphill may have too little clearance. Those levels affect whether there is room for a step, landing or ramp.

How does a slope affect planning height in Slough?

For an outbuilding that qualifies under householder permitted development in England, the height is measured from the highest ground level immediately next to the building to its highest point. If any part of the building is within two metres of the boundary, the overall height limit is 2.5 metres under those rules. When comparing a shed's stated height with that limit, account for where the shed will sit once the base is built. A reading from the lowest corner of the garden can give a different figure from the measurement used in the guidance.

If the proposal involves substantial changes to ground levels or retaining work, check with Slough Borough Council how it would treat the whole proposal before deciding on the finished height. If the property is outside the borough, check with the council responsible for that address. The national rules and their exceptions are set out in the GOV.UK technical guidance on householder permitted development.
